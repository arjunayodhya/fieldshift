              Understand the data

              What the words mean
              Evapotranspiration (ET) and ET0
              Water that leaves a field, both by evaporating from the soil and by passing through the plants. ET0 is the standard yardstick: how much a well-watered grass field would lose. Hotter, drier, windier, sunnier days mean higher ET0.
              Water balance
              Water coming in (rain, snow, irrigation) minus water going out (ET). Here rain covers about a tenth of what the air takes, so irrigation fills the rest.
              NDVI (greenness index)
              A satellite measure of how much healthy green leaf covers the ground. Near 0 is bare soil; 0.8 or more is a dense green crop. It drops after each hay cutting and when plants are stressed.
              Soil moisture, and "wetter or drier than normal"
              How much of the soil is water. A soil moisture anomaly is the difference from what is usual for that time of year, which is how a dry spell shows up.
              Frost-free season and frost risk
              The stretch between the last spring frost and the first fall frost. Frost-tender crops like teff and sorghum-sudangrass have to fit inside it.
              Growing degree days (GDD)
              A running total of warmth above the temperature where a crop starts growing. More GDD means faster growth and an earlier harvest.
              Soil salt (EC)
              Electrical conductivity, a measure of salt in the soil. Each crop has a level above which yield starts to fall.
              Nitrogen-fixing crop (legume)
              Crops like alfalfa host bacteria that take nitrogen from the air. They need little nitrogen fertilizer and leave some behind for the next crop.
              Cover crop
              A crop grown mainly to keep the ground covered between cash crops. It holds soil against wind and water and adds organic matter.
              Deep-rooted crop
              Roots that reach several feet down open channels for water and air, and reach water that shallow crops can't.
              Drought- and heat-tolerant crop
              Crops such as sorghum-sudangrass and teff keep growing in heat that slows alfalfa, and some can pause in a dry spell and recover.
              Satellite numbers vs numbers measured on the ground

              Satellites and weather models cover every place and every year, but they see large squares, not your field. Ground measurements are exact for one spot but sparse. Using both shows where to trust each. Three examples from this site:

              • Rain: the NASA weather model says about 8 in a year here. The GPM IMERG satellite says about 5.6 in, and the Fallon weather station records about 5 in. So this page uses the satellite for rain.
              • Crop greenness: the first satellite point we tried fell on the town of Fallon and read like bare ground. A USDA crop map was needed to find a real alfalfa field. A satellite number means little until you know what is on the ground there.
              • Soil: SMAP senses only the top 2 in of soil over a 6-mile square. The USDA soil survey, made by people digging pits, tells you texture, salt and depth for a mapped field. A lab test of your own soil beats both.

              Where they disagree, or where no ground number exists, this page says so in the data gaps.

              Know the limits of this data

                This tool supports your judgment; it does not replace it. It finds patterns in NASA and USDA data and lays out options. You know your field, water rights, equipment and buyers. Before changing a rotation, talk with your extension office or a local agronomist, especially about the items listed under "Risks" and "Data gaps".
